# ED Authority and Accountability

The Executive Director role is defined in three separate governance documents with meaningfully different scopes. This note maps how the three definitions relate to each other and identifies where they create tension or ambiguity.

---

## How the Three Documents Define the ED Role

### Definition 1 — Bylaws §19 (Administrative)
> "The Executive Director shall:  
> (a) ensure proper records of all meetings of members and of the Board of Directors are kept,  
> (b) have charge of the books, records and correspondence of the Board of Directors,  
> (c) have custody of the Association seal and shall co-sign with the President all documents, contracts and instruments in writing requiring the official signature of the Association."

**Scope:** Administrative record-keeping and co-signing authority. Nothing more. The Bylaws frame the ED as a secretary/administrator, not an organizational leader.

---

### Definition 2 — Board Policy Manual / Carver (Operational Authority)
The BEDR section grants the ED comprehensive operational authority within the Ends and Boundaries defined by the Board:

> "As long as the ED uses any reasonable interpretation of the Board's Ends and Executive Boundaries, the ED is authorized to establish all further operational policies, make all decisions, take all actions, establish all practices, and develop all activities." (BEDR-C1)

**Scope:** Full operational authority for everything not explicitly prohibited. The ED is the Board's only link to operational achievement (BEDR-B). The Board monitors outcomes, not methods.

## The Tension Between Definitions 1 and 2

The Bylaws and the Board Policy Manual tell different stories about the ED. This is not unusual for a Carver-governed organization — the Bylaws were written before the Carver model was adopted, and the Carver model is the operative governance framework. However, there is a legal dimension to consider:

- **If a dispute ever arose about the scope of the ED's authority**, a strict reading of the Bylaws would suggest the ED has only administrative authority. The Board Policy Manual's delegation (BEDR-C1) is an internal governance document — it may not bind third parties.
- **The signing authority provision** (Bylaws §34) gives the ED co-signing authority for financial transactions, which is broader than the §19 description suggests. This internal inconsistency within the Bylaws themselves hints at incremental amendments without full harmonization.

**Recommendation:** The Bylaws should be amended to include a general clause describing the ED as the organization's chief operating officer with authority delegated by the Board, consistent with the Carver model. This would bring §19 into alignment with the actual governance practice.

## The Volume of ED Accountabilities vs. the Size of the Organization

The operational policies assign the ED responsibility for risk management, DEI monitoring, bilingual compliance, budget preparation, screening administration, privacy breach response, insurance management, contract review, crisis communication, and complaint triage — among others.

For a small provincial sport organization with a membership of ~289 and likely a single part-time or full-time ED (and possibly no other staff), this is an exceptionally heavy accountability load. Some observations:

1. **Single point of failure**: Almost every governance function flows through the ED. If the ED role is vacant or the incumbent is the subject of a complaint, many governance functions have no clear backup. (Note: [[2.18 Internal Dispute Resolution]] says conflicts involving the ED are reported to the President — that one backup is covered.)

2. **The complaint triage 24-hour rule** is particularly demanding for a part-time ED. During busy tournament seasons or summer program periods, a 24-hour turnaround on complaint triage requires reliable availability.

3. **The annual monitoring cycle** from the [[Board Policy Manual (Feb 2024)]] (p. 12) compounds this: the ED is expected to produce semi-annual reports on member treatment, quarterly financial reports, quarterly communication and support reports, and annual reports on all GE Ends. Whether these monitoring reports are actually produced is unknown from this vault.

## The Board's Role in ED Accountability

Under the Carver model, the Board monitors the ED against Ends and Boundaries, not against activities. The monitoring schedule specifies HOW the Board does this. Two key checks:

- **EDB-A (member treatment)**: Internal report, semi-annually
- **EDB-B (financial)**: Internal quarterly + external annually

The Board's relationship with the ED is structured so that it deals with the ED "as a whole" (BEDR-E) — meaning individual Board members should not be giving the ED directions or using the ED to advance their own agendas. This is important protection for the ED in a small organization where boundary confusion between Board members and operational management is common.

## Signing Authority Cross-Check

The Bylaws (§34) and the Financial Management Policy (2.6) both address signing authority:

| Source | Rule |
|--------|------|
| Bylaws §34 | 2 of 3 designated signatories; one must be ED or President |
| Bylaws §17 | President co-signs with any other Director |
| Bylaws §19 | ED co-signs with the President |
| [[2.6 Financial Management]] | Dual-signature: ED and one Board member for all payments and cheques |

The Bylaws and 2.6 are consistent but the description in 2.6 ("ED and one Board member") is slightly narrower than the Bylaws ("2 of 3 designated signatories"). In practice this means any Board member could serve as the second signatory, not just one specific one — a reasonable flexibility.
